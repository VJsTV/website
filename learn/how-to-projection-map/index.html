How to Projection Map

A practical, end-to-end workflow for projecting moving image onto a non-flat surface — the same workflow used at festivals and architectural installations, scaled down to a project you can do this weekend.

Step 1 — Scout the surface

Before you buy anything, photograph the surface in the lighting conditions you will project in. Note its material (matte, reflective, dark, transparent), how far you can place a projector from it, and what ambient light will be present. Dark, matte, indoor surfaces are easy. Reflective glass at sunset is hard.

Step 2 — Choose the projector

The two numbers that matter are lumens (brightness) and throw ratio (how far the projector sits from the surface for a given image size). For a small interior installation, 3000–5000 lumens is fine. For an exterior building facade in a partially lit street, you usually need 20 000+ lumens, often stacked. Measure the surface, then use the projector manufacturer's throw calculator.

Step 3 — Build the geometry in software

Open MadMapper or Resolume Arena, create a new project at the projector's native resolution, and trace the surface. In MadMapper this means dragging quad surfaces and snapping their corners to the physical edges. In Resolume you use the Output screen with slices. Every surface gets its own input feed.

Step 4 — Make content that respects the surface

Generic loops mapped onto a building look like loops mapped onto a building. The work that travels — the work you see at festivals — uses content custom-designed for the surface: animations that "open" architectural features, light that appears to be inside the surface, sequences that step around the corners of the geometry. Reference work in our projects directory.

Step 5 — Test in-place at night

The first 80% of mapping happens at the desk. The last 20% — the part audiences notice — happens on site, in the dark, with the projector physically in its final position. Bring a laptop, a long HDMI cable, and at least an hour of unhurried tweak time before doors. Save your project file.

Step 6 — Run the show

Triggering can be timecoded (locked to music) or live-VJ'd. For installations, schedule the playback. For live performance, route MIDI or OSC from your audio source to the mapping software. Always have a backup laptop or at least a saved file on a USB stick — projectors and laptops both fail at exactly the wrong moment.

Frequently asked

What software is best for projection mapping?

For small to medium projects, MadMapper is purpose-built and has the cleanest workflow. For larger shows that combine mapping with live VJing, Resolume Arena is the standard. For broadcast and tour-grade installations, Disguise or Pandoras Box.

How many lumens do I need?

Indoor / dark room: 3000–5000 lm. Indoor / partial ambient light: 8000–12 000 lm. Outdoor at night, small building: 15 000–20 000 lm. Outdoor at night, large facade: stacked 30 000+ lm. The number is multiplicative with surface area, so always sanity-check on a calculator.

Can I projection-map without a powerful computer?

For static loops on a small surface, yes — most modern laptops will play back HD video into a single projector. For multi-projector mapping, real-time content, or 4K output, you need a discrete GPU. The bottleneck is almost always video memory and decode performance, not CPU.

What is the difference between projection mapping and 3D mapping?

"Projection mapping" is the umbrella term — fitting an image to a non-flat surface. "3D mapping" usually means content that gives the illusion of three-dimensional depth on a 2D facade (rotating geometry, openings into virtual space). 3D mapping is a technique within projection mapping.
